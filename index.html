U.S. SEC filing · Reuters · 2 Nov 2026 · Material follow-up to 28 Oct IPO registration
4.9 / 5

Anthropic’s amended IPO filing puts a $895bn compute bill in view

The new S-1 amendment reports nearly $900bn in aggregate compute and infrastructure commitments, including $69.8bn due within a year. It also shows revenue growth slowing sharply in the third quarter.

FOLLOW-UP: SIGNAL covered Anthropic’s initial IPO registration on 28 October. The 2 November amendment adds Q3 financials, a much fuller account of long-dated compute commitments and a warning that some capacity partners rely on outside financing. Read the earlier SIGNAL issue ↗

Anthropic’s amended S-1 says revenue reached $16.96bn in the third quarter, up 47% from the previous quarter. That is still rapid growth, but far below the 144% quarter-on-quarter increase reported for Q2. Net loss narrowed to $23.29bn from $66.3bn in Q2, while the company recorded a $567m operating loss. The filing reports $33.24bn in revenue and $104.04bn in net loss for the first nine months of 2026. The filing attributes $94.3bn of that nine-month loss to fair-value changes in convertible notes, derivative liabilities and simple agreements for future equity, primarily driven by increases in Anthropic’s valuation. That accounting loss should not be read as $104bn of operating cash burn.

The number investors will focus on is the $895.4bn aggregate value of purchase commitments and lease agreements, substantially all tied to compute and infrastructure. Anthropic expects about $447.5bn of that amount to be incurred before the end of 2030; approximately $69.8bn is due in the 12 months after 30 September. These are long-term commitments, including leases not yet commenced, not a bill payable today. The filing also says $213.8bn of the aggregate obligations is subject to exercisable termination rights or conditions before commencement, including counterparties’ ability to secure financing, facilities or regulatory approvals. At 30 September, Anthropic reported $131.845bn in cash, cash equivalents and short-term investments, plus $14.8bn available under a credit facility. The headline commitment total is therefore large, but not every dollar is unconditional or due today.

Why developers should care: Frontier-model access depends on more than a model roadmap. Compute leases, chip supply and the creditworthiness of cloud partners all affect capacity, regional availability and eventually API economics.

Why it leads: The filing puts the growth curve and the infrastructure obligations behind it in the same document. It is the clearest new evidence yet of how capital-intensive frontier AI has become.

Marvell / Lumentum 8-K · Reuters · 2 Nov 2026 · Material follow-up
4.8 / 5

Marvell agrees to buy Lumentum in a $115bn AI-connectivity deal

The definitive all-stock deal joins Marvell’s electrical interconnect and switching business with Lumentum’s lasers, optical transceivers and photonics manufacturing.

FOLLOW-UP: The 29 October SIGNAL issue reported advanced talks. The new development is a definitive agreement: Lumentum shareholders will receive 4.5 Marvell shares per share and own about 33% of the combined company. Read the earlier SIGNAL issue ↗

Reuters values the transaction at $115bn including debt, with Lumentum’s equity valued at $102.5bn. The companies say the combination will span silicon photonics, compound semiconductors, optical circuit switching, advanced packaging and specialist manufacturing. They project a $550bn addressable market and more than $1bn in annual run-rate cost synergies; those are company estimates, not achieved results. Closing is expected in the first half of 2028, subject to approvals. Marvell shares fell more than 10% and Lumentum’s more than 15% after the announcement, a reminder that investors see substantial valuation and integration risk.

Why developers should care: Optical links are becoming a critical part of large AI clusters: thousands of accelerators need fast, power-efficient connections. The deal could change the supplier landscape for data-centre networking, silicon photonics and co-designed systems.

Why it ranks here: A large, definitive transaction aimed at a real infrastructure bottleneck. The long regulatory and integration runway keeps execution risk high.

Original sources: Marvell 8-K / transaction exhibit ↗ · Lumentum announcement ↗ · Reuters ↗

Reuters · 2 Nov 2026 · Bond sale reported from bank memo and SEC filing
4.7 / 5

Alphabet sells €6bn of bonds as AI infrastructure strains the debt market

Demand topped €17bn for a seven-part euro offering, while Alphabet’s data-centre capex and future lease commitments keep investors focused on the cost of the AI build-out.

Alphabet launched a €6bn bond sale in seven tranches, with maturities from two to 19 years and total demand just over €17bn, Reuters reported from a bank memo. The company said proceeds would be used for general corporate purposes and could include debt repayment; the sale is not earmarked solely for AI. Alphabet expects capital spending of up to $205bn this year. As of 30 September, it had also signed data-centre-heavy leases with $130.5bn in future payments that had not yet commenced and were not on its balance sheet.

Why developers should care: Cloud capacity, regional availability and accelerator supply depend on financing as well as demand. The cost of capital can influence how quickly hyperscalers build, lease and expand compute.

Why it ranks here: A concrete, large financing transaction with strong order demand, set against unusually large infrastructure commitments.

Original sources: Reuters ↗

Reuters · 2 Nov 2026 · Regulatory filings reported
4.6 / 5

Oracle seeks $20bn from bond and share sales for its next expansion

Oracle plans a $20bn capital raise as it expands AI and cloud infrastructure. The proceeds are for general corporate purposes, not a ring-fenced AI budget.

Oracle plans to raise $20bn through bond and share sales, Reuters reported from regulatory filings. The bond offering comprises six tranches with maturities from 2029 to 2046; final prices and terms were not yet set. Oracle said proceeds could support capital spending, debt repayment, acquisitions, dividends and share buybacks. It had $125.34bn in notes payable and other borrowings at 31 August. The company has said capital spending will peak this fiscal year and next, expects revenue above $225bn in fiscal 2030 and forecasts positive free cash flow in the second half of fiscal 2028.

Why developers should care: Oracle is a major AI infrastructure supplier and a key capacity partner for large model developers. Its financing costs and ability to convert data-centre spending into cash flow can affect how quickly capacity is delivered.

Why it ranks here: A fresh capital-raising plan from a central AI infrastructure provider; the general-purpose use of proceeds and debt exposure need to be kept in view.

Original sources: Reuters ↗
05
OpenAI · The Verge · 2 Nov 2026 · Company programme and Georgia agreement
4.5 / 5

OpenAI makes grid flexibility part of the bargain for new compute campuses

The Grid Protectors programme will ask future campuses to reduce electricity demand when local grids are under strain. A Georgia Power agreement includes up to 1,000 MW of flexible demand response.

OpenAI says it will work with utilities, regulators and policymakers on arrangements that let compute campuses turn down demand during periods of grid stress. For its planned Effingham County, Georgia campus, the company says it will pay the full infrastructure and electric-service costs required for the project. Its agreement with Georgia Power includes up to 1,000 MW of flexible demand response, which OpenAI equates to the average demand of roughly 700,000 households. The campus is still in development and is intended to be the programme’s pilot. OpenAI says batteries, on-site generation and power controls may also be used where appropriate. The company acknowledges that demand flexibility cannot replace investment in new generation and transmission.

Why developers should care: Power is now a deployment constraint alongside chips and networking. Flexible-load agreements could influence where data centres are approved, how they are operated and whether utilities pass grid costs to other customers.

Why it ranks here: A concrete response to grid pressure and community opposition. The promised flexibility is not yet a measured operating result; the campus is still being built.

Original sources: OpenAI programme announcement ↗ · The Verge ↗
06
ADNOC · Reuters · The National · 2 Nov 2026 · Multi-year partnership announced
4.4 / 5

ADNOC will use OpenAI agents to connect oilfield planning with commercial decisions

The first deployments will combine field-development planning with decisions spanning production, refining, logistics, sales and trading, using UAE-based G42 compute.

ADNOC, OpenAI and G42 announced a multi-year partnership to deploy OpenAI Frontier across complex energy operations. The initial field-planning application is intended to connect decades of data from thousands of wells, multiple reservoirs and hundreds of surface facilities. ADNOC says the system could prepare and optimise development plans in hours rather than months. A second application will link decisions across production, processing, refining, logistics, sales and trading, including forecasts of supply, demand, prices and margins. ADNOC says ChatGPT Enterprise has already been rolled out to more than 40,000 employees, with adoption above 97%; those figures are company-reported. The partners say human specialists will retain oversight. No contract value or firm deployment timetable was disclosed.

Why developers should care: This is a high-consequence agent deployment, where connectors, data quality, permissions, audit trails and human approval matter as much as model capability. The useful test will be measurable improvements in planning and operating decisions.

Why it ranks here: One of the clearer industrial deployments of coordinated agents, though the promised speed gains and adoption figures remain company claims until measured independently.

  • Compute finance: model access is tied to long leases, partner credit and hyperscaler borrowing.

AI in context · analysis, not reported fact

Compute is becoming a balance-sheet problem

Anthropic’s long-dated commitments, Alphabet’s euro bond sale and Oracle’s $20bn raise all point to the same constraint: the AI build-out has to be financed before its revenue arrives. The ECB and China’s regulator are now describing the concentration of capital and debt as a financial-stability concern. That does not prove an AI bubble; it does mean capacity plans are exposed to credit conditions as well as chip supply.

Connectivity and power are moving up the stack

Marvell’s Lumentum deal is a bet on optical links and tighter electrical/optical co-design. OpenAI’s grid programme addresses the other physical limit: electricity at the site boundary. As clusters grow, the practical ceiling is increasingly set by moving data and securing power, not by accelerator specifications alone.

Industrial agents will be judged on control, not demos

ADNOC’s deployment shows how agent systems are being positioned for workflows that cross operations, capital and trading. The value will depend on reliable access to operational data, traceable decisions, permission boundaries and human intervention when a recommendation has physical or financial consequences. The hours-versus-months claim still needs operating evidence.
