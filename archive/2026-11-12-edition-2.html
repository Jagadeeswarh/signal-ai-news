01CHIPS / CORPORATE GOVERNANCEFOLLOW-UP11 NOV 2026 · 22:34 UTC

Nvidia’s board is reportedly weighing Jensen Huang’s removal. No decision is public.

Reuters says directors are meeting to decide whether to oust the CEO. This is a sharp escalation from the independent investigation announced earlier in the week, but it is not a confirmed board decision.

Reuters reported late Wednesday that Nvidia’s board was meeting to decide whether to remove Huang, citing one person familiar with the matter. The report followed a Wall Street Journal account that the company was preparing to fire him; Reuters also said Bloomberg had reported that the board was working with an outside firm to identify potential replacements. Nvidia did not immediately respond to Reuters’ request for comment.

The confirmed process is narrower. On 10 November, the independent directors said they had retained former acting U.S. Attorney Joon H. Kim and Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz to conduct the investigation announced on Sunday. The board had expressed confidence in Huang earlier in the week. No public vote, resignation or succession announcement had been made by this edition’s 08:39 IST cutoff.

Reuters reported that Nvidia shares fell more than 8% in extended trading after the Journal report. The company’s market value was down about $600 billion that week if those losses held. That is a market estimate, not a completed loss, and the board’s deliberations remain sourced reporting rather than a company-confirmed outcome.

Evidence check: Reuters’ board-meeting report relies on a source familiar with the matter and says Nvidia did not respond. Nvidia’s 10 November statement confirms the independent investigation and outside counsel, not a decision to remove Huang. The underlying allegations have not been established as findings.

Why developers should care: Nvidia’s roadmap, software ecosystem and customer relationships are unusually founder-centred. A leadership change at the company supplying much of the AI accelerator market could affect product priorities, customer confidence and platform decisions, even if operations remain stable.

Why it ranks first: the potential governance shock touches the central supplier in the AI compute stack. The consequence is enormous, but the probability and timing remain uncertain.

Sources: Reuters · 11 Nov · NVIDIA board statement · 10 Nov · Earlier SIGNAL coverage · 11 Nov
02EXPORT CONTROLS / U.S.–CHINAFOLLOW-UP11 NOV 2026 · 21:21 UTC

Axios reports a third Trump-approved chip-diversion crackdown is still stalled.

The reported dispute is inside the U.S. government: President Donald Trump approved action against companies suspected of diverting Nvidia chips to China, while Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick allegedly resisted implementation.

Axios, citing six high-level sources briefed on the discussions, reported that Trump authorised a crackdown for the third time this year, but Lutnick repeatedly delayed action. At a Situation Room meeting, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent challenged the Commerce Department’s resistance. Axios says Trump later agreed to proceed, but Lutnick proposed developing more options rather than executing the decision.

Reuters carried the report on 11 November. The Commerce Department spokesperson denied that Bessent confronted Lutnick and did not immediately comment on the other allegations. Reuters said the White House and Lutnick’s spokesperson did not immediately respond. No new Entity List action or published enforcement order accompanied the report.

Evidence check: This is an account of internal deliberations from anonymous sources, not an official government record. The denial of the specific confrontation matters; the wider claim of repeated delays remains attributed to Axios.

Why developers should care: Export controls only work if licensing, end-use checks and enforcement keep pace with the hardware market. A policy that exists on paper but is not implemented creates uncertainty for cloud providers, chip vendors and teams planning cross-border infrastructure.

Why it ranks here: it exposes a possible gap between U.S. policy intent and execution while access to advanced compute remains a strategic issue.

Sources: Axios · 11 Nov · Reuters · 11 Nov · Earlier SIGNAL coverage · 11 Nov
03CHIPS / CHINA / ENFORCEMENTNEW11 NOV 2026 · 05:13 UTC

A blacklisted Chinese university bought Nvidia H200 chips through local suppliers, records show.

Reuters found procurement records showing that Northwestern Polytechnical University purchased two H200 processors and rented access to Nvidia-powered servers despite U.S. restrictions on the institution.

The university paid 874,700 yuan (about $123,000) for two H200 chips in October, according to a procurement record. The purchase was made from a Chinese supplier, not directly from Nvidia. Reuters also found contracts for rented compute containing H200s, A100s and A800s, including work described as model fine-tuning and reinforcement-learning training.

Northwestern Polytechnical is on the U.S. Entity List and is one of China’s “Seven Sons of National Defense” universities. Reuters reviewed 26 procurement and corporate records and found other Chinese universities and research institutions buying or renting access to restricted Nvidia hardware through domestic suppliers.

Evidence check: Reuters could not determine where the processors originated or where the rented servers operated. The records do not establish that Nvidia sold these chips to the university or prove a specific export-control violation. The university, Nvidia and the U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security did not respond to Reuters.

Why developers should care: The real constraint on AI compute is not only which chips a country can buy directly. Reseller networks, server rentals and remote access can create paths around restrictions, complicating compliance for cloud platforms and research institutions.

Why it ranks here: this is documentary evidence of access to restricted compute, rather than a policy debate or company forecast. The supply route is still unresolved.

Sources: Reuters investigation · 11 Nov
04POWER / DATA CENTRES / INDIANEW11 NOV 2026

BloombergNEF sees data-centre electricity demand almost quadrupling by 2035.

The more immediate warning is about getting power quickly: BNEF has tracked 181GW of announced gas-fired generation proposed alongside data centres, more than 90% of it in the United States.

BloombergNEF’s new report expects data-centre electricity demand to reach 2,354 terawatt-hours by 2035, equivalent to 7% of global electricity use. Grid-connection delays in the United States and Europe are pushing some developers toward on-site gas generation while they wait for utility capacity.

The 181GW figure is a pipeline of announced proposals, not a forecast that all plants will be built. BNEF estimates that if every U.S. on-site gas project in its dataset were built and operated under its assumptions, annual emissions would be about 345 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent, 152Mt more than supplying the associated data-centre capacity with average U.S. grid electricity. BNEF warns that temporary gas plants could remain in service if their economics make them attractive.

India is a particularly important part of the forecast. BNEF expects data-centre electricity demand to rise from 11TWh in 2025 to 91TWh in 2035. Under its Economic Transition Scenario, existing coal plants supply much of the near-term increase, though bilateral and captive clean-power projects can change the mix.

Evidence check: These are BNEF scenarios and a tracked project pipeline, not metered demand or committed construction. The report was commissioned by Bloomberg Philanthropies.

Why developers should care: GPU availability is only one part of capacity planning. Grid access, transmission, cooling and long-term power contracts determine when a data centre can actually run inference workloads. India’s 91TWh projection makes power procurement and additional clean generation a strategic issue, not a facilities footnote.

Why it ranks here: power is becoming a hard limit on AI deployment. The report connects global demand, fossil-fuel lock-in and India’s rapidly growing data-centre footprint.

Sources: BloombergNEF · 11 Nov
05CHINA / SEMICONDUCTORS / CAPITALNEW10 NOV 2026

Alibaba gets the green light to move T-Head toward a Hong Kong listing.

T-Head, Alibaba’s wholly owned chip-design subsidiary, has filed confidentially for a proposed separate listing. The move could give one of China’s largest in-house chip programmes a more visible capital base.

Alibaba’s Hong Kong filing says the exchange confirmed that the group may proceed with the proposed spin-off. T-Head submitted a confidential Form A1 listing application on 11 September, seeking permission to list H shares on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange. The proposed global offering would include a Hong Kong public tranche and an institutional placement.

The filing does not set a fundraising target, valuation, share count or timetable. The transaction remains subject to the relevant listing and regulatory approvals. T-Head is a wholly owned subsidiary today; a proposed spin-off is not the same as a completed listing or a change in control.

Evidence check: The facts above come from Alibaba’s own exchange filing and Reuters’ report. This is evidence of a financing and corporate-structure step, not evidence that T-Head’s chips match Nvidia in performance, software maturity or volume availability.

Why developers should care: China’s domestic accelerator ecosystem needs more than silicon. A separately financed T-Head could invest in design, compiler support, software libraries and customer qualification. Those are the pieces that determine whether local hardware becomes a practical platform.

Why it ranks here: it is a concrete capital-markets development in a strategically important Chinese chip supplier, although the eventual offering terms and technical impact remain unknown.

Sources: Alibaba Group / HKEX filing · 10 Nov · Reuters · 10 Nov
06MEMORY / HBM / SOUTH KOREANEW12 NOV 2026 · 01:43 UTC

Samsung breaks ground on a ₩6tn HBM packaging plant and pulls the start date forward.

South Korea’s president says the Asan project will be followed by about ₩40tn in advanced-equipment investment. Samsung’s new line is now scheduled to begin operating in June 2028, a year earlier than first planned.

President Lee Jae Myung said Samsung Electronics’ 6 trillion won (about $4.48bn) investment in a high-bandwidth memory packaging plant in Asan would be followed by around 40 trillion won in advanced-equipment investment. The remarks came at a groundbreaking ceremony for part of investment plans Samsung affiliates announced in July.

Samsung’s presentation puts the new HBM line’s planned start in June 2028. The facility is designed for server-memory demand and will have three floors of clean-room space covering about 31,000 square metres. The schedule is a company plan, not current production capacity.

Evidence check: Reuters reports the president’s remarks and Samsung’s presentation. The ₩40tn figure is additional advanced-equipment investment cited by Lee; it should not be read as the cost of the Asan plant alone.

Why developers should care: HBM supply and advanced packaging determine how many accelerators can be shipped as complete systems. More Korean capacity matters to the whole AI market, but the benefit arrives on a multi-year construction and qualification schedule.

Why it ranks here: this is a specific, dated expansion milestone in the memory and packaging bottleneck, not just another broad investment pledge.

Sources: Reuters · 12 Nov
07NETWORKING / U.S.–CHINA POLICYNEW10 NOV 2026 · 11:02 UTC

Microsoft is lobbying against a possible ban on Chinese optical transceivers.

The FCC is considering restrictions on new Chinese models. Cloud providers warn that cutting off a dominant supplier could delay data-centre construction even when the AI chips are already bought.

Reuters reports that Microsoft and industry groups have urged the Trump administration not to bar imports of new Chinese optical transceivers. These modules convert electrical signals to light and carry data over fibre between servers, switches and routers. Some large data centres need millions of them.

China accounts for about two-thirds of the global transceiver market, according to Counterpoint Research cited by Reuters. U.S. suppliers Coherent and Lumentum compete in the category but do not yet have enough scale to replace Chinese supply quickly, according to a Foundation for American Innovation report. The FCC has not publicly announced a rule. A possible 3.2-terabit-per-second threshold is under consideration, according to Reuters’ sources and a Morgan Stanley note; products at that speed are expected to ramp in 2028, with wider installation in 2029.

Evidence check: The lobbying is reported by Reuters from three people familiar with the matter. The proposed threshold is not an FCC decision, and no final scope or effective date has been published. Microsoft, the White House and the FCC did not comment to Reuters.

Why developers should care: A GPU cluster is only as useful as the network connecting it. This is a reminder that AI capacity depends on optics, switches and fibre as much as on accelerators, memory and power.

Why it ranks here: the story reveals a supply-chain choke point where national-security policy and the practical pace of AI infrastructure are in direct tension.

Sources: Reuters exclusive · 10 Nov
08INFERENCE SILICON / FUNDINGNEW11 NOV 2026 · 14:53 UTC

UK inference-chip startup Fractile raises $720m at a $7bn valuation.

The round includes the British government’s Sovereign AI fund and British Business Bank. Fractile says its architecture is aimed at faster, cheaper inference; its first commercial chip is planned for 2027.

Reuters reports that London- and Bristol-based Fractile raised $720m in a Series C led by Thrive Capital, Founders Fund, Lightspeed and Redpoint. Other investors include CapitalG, BlackRock, DST Global, Balderton, SV Angel, the UK Sovereign AI fund and British Business Bank.

Fractile is developing chips for inference, the stage when a trained model generates responses. The company says its design can improve speed while reducing energy use and cost. It plans to launch its first commercial chip next year.

Evidence check: The company has not yet shipped a commercial processor. The funding report did not include independent benchmark results, production yields or customer deployments. The valuation reflects investor expectations, not demonstrated market share.

Why developers should care: Inference economics increasingly shape the cost of running agents and production applications. A credible alternative would need competitive memory bandwidth, compiler support, model kernels and reliable supply—not just a promising chip design.

Why it ranks here: the round is unusually large for a UK chip startup and backs a strategic attempt to diversify inference hardware, but technical proof is still ahead.

Sources: Reuters · 11 Nov
09AI FOR SCIENCE / GENOMICSNEW11 NOV 2026

AlphaGenome 2 moves DNA prediction from broad tissues to specific cell states.

Google DeepMind says the model was trained on more than 107 million cell profiles across 68 tissues and can better identify genetic variants that matter in particular cell types.

AlphaGenome 2 takes a DNA sequence and a biological condition—such as a tissue, cell type or state—and predicts how variants may affect gene regulation. That conditional design is a change from the first AlphaGenome model, which predicted across broad tissue averages in parallel. DeepMind says the new model can also infer missing experimental readouts, such as chromatin accessibility from gene-activity measurements.

On the TenK10K single-cell dataset, DeepMind reports that AlphaGenome 2 identified 30% of causal variants versus 16% for its earlier model and other specialist tools, and directional gene effects in 43% versus 23%. The company also says the model reproduced a published finding about a regulatory variant affecting ETS2 in gut macrophages.

Evidence check: These benchmark comparisons are from DeepMind’s announcement. The model is available through an API for non-commercial research and in private preview for commercial research. DeepMind says it is for research use and has not been validated or approved for clinical or diagnostic purposes. Nature’s coverage adds independent scientific context, but the full article was not accessible during this run.

Why developers should care: This is an example of a specialised model becoming useful when paired with rich domain data and an API, rather than trying to solve biology with a general chatbot. The next test is whether external labs can reproduce the cell-specific gains on their own datasets.

Why it ranks here: it connects large-scale AI modelling to a difficult scientific problem—linking genetic variants to the cells where disease mechanisms actually operate.

Sources: Google DeepMind · 11 Nov · Nature · 11 Nov (full text unavailable)
10ROBOTICS / PHYSICAL AI / JAPANNEW11 NOV 2026

NTT’s world model screens robot actions before they happen.

The Japanese telecom group says a physics-aware adapter improved a small robot’s shelf-loading success rate from 73.1% to 92.3% by predicting drops and collapsing loads before the robot moved.

NTT has integrated a “physics control adapter” into a video-generation model. It estimates object properties such as mass, position, orientation and velocity, then uses equations of motion to predict what may happen under different candidate robot actions. A controller can compare those predicted futures and choose an action less likely to drop an item or topple a stack.

In NTT’s shelf-loading test with a small robot, success rose from 73.1% without the technology to 92.3% with it. The company also reports better results than conventional methods on the public OBJ3D video-prediction benchmark. Its first planned use is airport baggage loading and unloading, with field trials to be advanced alongside robotics company Mujin.

Evidence check: These are NTT’s results. The release does not state the number of trials, the range of objects tested or a real-world airport deployment. Field trials are planned; this is not yet a production system.

Why developers should care: The design combines a learned video model with explicit physical quantities. That can make a robot’s prediction more legible and useful to a controller than a plausible-looking video alone. The hard question is how well it transfers to messy, unfamiliar environments.

Why it ranks here: it is a concrete step from generative models toward action planning in the physical world, with a measurable—but still small-scale—robot test.

Sources: NTT · 11 Nov

AI IN CONTEXT

Analysis · not a reported fact

1 · The bottleneck is now the whole data-centre stack.

Samsung’s HBM packaging expansion, Microsoft’s optical-transceiver lobbying, AMD’s server CPUs and BNEF’s power forecast point to the same operational reality: accelerators do not become usable capacity until memory, networking, cooling and electricity arrive together. A shortage in any one layer can strand investment in the others.

2 · China’s chip ecosystem is building capital routes while controls strain.

T-Head’s proposed spin-off and the Hosin/Amicro listings show domestic chip firms turning to public markets. At the same time, Reuters’ H200 procurement records and Axios’ account of delayed enforcement show why export restrictions cannot be assessed from policy text alone. Neither listing proves that China has closed the performance or software gap.

3 · Agents are turning into a cost and distribution problem.

United’s restrictions, JetBlue’s more open stance, OpenAI’s Odessia acquisition, Adobe’s local MCP integrations and Google’s Tabletop experiment all touch the question of who can act on a user’s behalf. McKinsey’s banking work adds the cost side: agents can multiply token use through agent-to-agent calls, so permission, observability, budgets and liability need to be designed together.

4 · Specialised models are moving closer to the physical and biological world.

AlphaGenome 2 conditions predictions on cell type and state; NTT’s world model adds explicit physical quantities to robot action prediction. Both are promising, but both need independent replication and evidence beyond the initial evaluation setup before their claims translate into routine scientific or industrial use.

DECODE THE JARGON

Plain-English reference
HBM
High-bandwidth memory, stacked DRAM placed close to an accelerator to feed data quickly.
DRAM
Dynamic random-access memory, the main memory technology used in many servers and HBM stacks.
H200
Nvidia’s high-end data-centre GPU generation; export access depends on U.S. licensing rules.
Inference
The stage when a trained model generates an answer, prediction or action.
Entity List
The U.S. Commerce Department’s list of organisations subject to export restrictions.
Optical transceiver
A module that converts electrical data into light and back so equipment can communicate over fibre.
T-Head
Alibaba’s chip-design subsidiary, developing processors for data-centre and other workloads.
Single-cell profile
A measurement of gene activity or other molecular features in an individual cell, rather than a tissue average.
TenK10K
A single-cell dataset used by DeepMind to evaluate genetic-variant predictions.
World model
A model that predicts how the environment may change after an action.
OBJ3D
A benchmark for evaluating how well a model predicts future video frames of 3D objects.
Physical AI
AI systems that perceive, plan and act through robots or other machines in the real world.
MCP
Model Context Protocol, a standard for connecting AI systems to tools, data and applications.
EPYC / Zen 6
AMD’s server CPU family and the processor architecture behind its new 6th-generation range.
AI FinOps
Financial-operations practices for measuring, routing and controlling AI usage and token costs.
TWh
Terawatt-hour, a unit of electricity consumption equal to one trillion watt-hours.
Agent
Software that can plan and carry out tasks using tools, data and permissions rather than only returning text.
Class certification
A court decision on whether a lawsuit can proceed on behalf of a defined group rather than only named plaintiffs.

Today’s strongest signal: the AI build-out is constrained by the systems around the GPU—memory packaging, optical networking, power and export enforcement.

Watch the difference between an announced project, a funded design and capacity that is actually installed and usable.

DEVELOPER WATCH

  • Map data-centre costs beyond accelerator rental: optics, memory, power and cooling are first-order variables.
  • For agents, model quality is only part of production readiness. Identity, scoped permissions, audit logs and transaction liability are now platform issues.
  • For physical AI, demand trial counts, latency, recovery behaviour and transfer to unfamiliar objects—not just one success-rate comparison.
  • For scientific models, check API terms, commercial access and whether external labs can reproduce benchmark gains.

ASIA / INDIA LENS

South Korea is accelerating HBM packaging capacity. Alibaba is taking T-Head toward a Hong Kong listing. Chinese robotics and memory-chip firms are also tapping Hong Kong markets. In India, BNEF’s data-centre demand forecast rises from 11TWh in 2025 to 91TWh by 2035; clean-power procurement and grid capacity will shape the outcome.
