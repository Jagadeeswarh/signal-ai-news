THE POWER, POLICY & SUPPLY-CHAIN EDITION

AI’s next constraint is increasingly the infrastructure around the chips

Anthropic is reportedly buying its way into a generator queue. Nvidia has opened a board investigation with national-security implications. Across Southeast Asia and Scotland, grid capacity, water and permits are shaping which AI data centres can actually be built.

01POWER / COMPUTE8 NOV 2026

Anthropic reportedly takes over 1.7GW of gas-generator orders for $3.4bn

The deal would secure equipment for on-site power. Piper Sandler independently lists a $3.4bn sale to an unnamed frontier AI lab.

The Financial Times reported on 8 November that Anthropic took over OnePWR Solutions commitments for 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty Caterpillar gas generators, in a transaction valued at about $3.4bn. Reuters carried the report but said it could not independently verify it. Anthropic and OnePWR did not respond to requests for comment. The reported package includes engineering and construction capabilities needed to install the equipment; Anthropic did not buy OnePWR itself and no OnePWR employees transferred.

Piper Sandler’s transaction page provides a narrower check: it lists a September 2026, $3.4bn sale of OnePWR’s power-generation platform to “a frontier AI lab”. It does not name Anthropic or confirm the 1.7GW figure. Anthropic’s 2 November S-1/A separately says some planned data centres rely on dedicated on-site gas generation and details grid-connection, generator, fuel and pipeline constraints.

Treat 1.7GW as reported generator nameplate capacity, not 1.7GW of commissioned electricity or usable data-centre load. Delivery dates, deployment sites and the exact split between equipment and installation work remain undisclosed. The public filing does not identify this transaction as a separate line item.

Evidence: The buyer attribution and generator quantity come from the FT report, relayed by Reuters; Reuters could not independently verify the deal. Piper Sandler confirms a $3.4bn platform sale to an unnamed frontier AI lab, not the buyer’s identity or generator count.

Why developers should care: Power is becoming a first-order constraint on model training and inference. Owning a place in the generator queue may reduce dependence on grid upgrades, but it also brings fuel, permitting, construction, emissions and operating risks into the AI company’s cost base.

Editorial rank: The strongest infrastructure signal of the window: a reported multi-billion-dollar move to secure physical power equipment. The ranking reflects its potential consequence, not certainty about every reported detail.

02CHIPS / GEOPOLITICS8 NOV 2026

Nvidia opens an independent probe into reported foreign-intelligence targeting of Jensen Huang

The board has commissioned outside counsel to examine allegations tied to a Singapore-based chip customer. Nvidia says it has no reason to believe secrets were compromised.

Nvidia said on 8 November that a committee of independent directors, assisted by outside counsel, would investigate reports that CEO Jensen Huang had been the target of a foreign-intelligence effort. The board’s statement followed reporting by The New York Times, described by Reuters and the Associated Press, about Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based data-centre company Megaspeed.

US authorities have scrutinised Megaspeed over suspicions involving access to advanced Nvidia chips restricted from export to China. The intelligence allegation is not a finding by Nvidia’s board, and the company has not said that proprietary information or national-security material was disclosed. Nvidia’s statement says the board has no reason to believe either was compromised. Huang supports the inquiry and the company says he will continue as CEO while it proceeds.

The immediate development is the formal board investigation. No new export-control rule, chip-sales suspension or finding of a security breach was announced on Sunday.

Evidence: Nvidia’s 8 November statement confirms the independent investigation and says there is no current reason to believe secrets or national security were compromised. The underlying intelligence claims are reported allegations, not established findings.

Why developers should care: Nvidia sits at the centre of the accelerator supply chain. The inquiry could sharpen scrutiny of customer due diligence, chip-diversion controls and the governance of a supplier whose leadership is unusually central to its strategy.

Editorial rank: Ranks second because the event touches the largest AI-accelerator supplier and US–China technology controls. The board action is confirmed; the alleged espionage and any compromise remain unproven.

Publisher / primary source: NVIDIA board statement · Reuters · Associated Press · NVIDIA Board — 8 Nov 2026 · Reuters — 8 Nov 2026 · Associated Press — 8 Nov 2026

03ASIA / DATA CENTRES7 NOV 2026

Southeast Asia’s data-centre boom is meeting power, water and permitting limits

New regional reporting connects Thailand’s project pause with a stop-work order in Indonesia. The underlying government actions happened in September, not this weekend.

A Guardian regional report published on 7 November describes how fast-growing data-centre plans across Southeast Asia are running into resource and permitting constraints. Thailand paused construction on 49 projects and deferred decisions on 117 more in September while authorities worked on rules for energy, water and project monitoring. Those numbers describe the September decision; the Guardian article is new reporting, not a new November moratorium.

In Indonesia, Reuters reported on 30 September that West Java’s governor ordered Singapore-based BDx to stop work on a planned 640MW AI campus at Jatiluhur until permits were obtained and the environmental-impact process completed. The governor said the issue was missing permits, not a finding that the project had violated environmental rules.

The Guardian also cites Wood Mackenzie research estimating that data centres already account for 24% of electricity consumption in Malaysia’s Johor state, potentially rising to 40% by 2035. That is an analyst estimate, not a grid-operator forecast. The region still has a large construction pipeline, but planned megawatts are not the same as permitted, powered capacity.

Evidence: The Guardian’s 7 November article reports the Thailand figures and cites Wood Mackenzie and Cushman & Wakefield. Reuters’ 30 September report documents the separate BDx stop-work order and the governor’s stated permitting rationale.

Why developers should care: For cloud and AI developers serving Asian customers, regional capacity plans depend on grid connections, water availability, environmental review and local consent. A campus announced at gigawatt scale may still be years from reliable service.

Editorial rank: Ranks third because Southeast Asia has become a major destination for new capacity. The story is a regional synthesis of September actions published within this edition’s 48-hour window, not a claim that all the pauses began this weekend.

Publisher / primary source: The Guardian regional investigation · Reuters background reporting · The Guardian — 7 Nov 2026 · Reuters on BDx / West Java — 30 Sep 2026 · Cushman & Wakefield APAC Data Centre H1 2026 update

04UK / GRID POWER7 NOV 2026

A 500MW Scottish AI campus is now planned to run from the grid, not private-wire renewables

DataVita has revised the power description for its Lanarkshire project while retaining a renewable power-purchase agreement claim.

DataVita has changed the immediate power plan for its proposed 500MW AI data-centre expansion near Chapelhall, Lanarkshire. The Guardian reported on 7 November that the company now describes the site as “fully grid served”, with wind and solar connections only a possibility for the future. Earlier material had promised more than 1GW of wind, solar and battery capacity delivered through private wires directly to the data centres.

DataVita says its grid supply will be covered by a renewable power-purchase agreement (PPA) and that its renewable commitment is unchanged. A PPA is a contractual arrangement for electricity; it is not the same thing as a dedicated private-wire connection from a specific wind or solar project. The distinction matters for physical delivery, grid access and how the project’s energy claims are assessed.

The UK government announced a £300m financing package for the wider AI Growth Zone in August, including a £202m National Wealth Fund guarantee. The Guardian says the proposed campus is expected to support 3,400 jobs. The revised grid-served plan does not itself cancel the project, but it makes grid connection timing and capacity central to delivery.

Evidence: The Guardian quotes DataVita’s revised description and compares it with the company’s earlier private-wire statement. GOV.UK’s 18 August release confirms the £300m package and £202m guarantee; job figures are government projections.

Why developers should care: This is a practical reminder to distinguish renewable procurement claims from physical power infrastructure. Grid connection queues, transmission upgrades and firm supply can determine when an AI campus becomes operational.

Editorial rank: Ranks fourth as a concrete project-level example of the gap between announced renewable capacity and the power connection a data centre will actually use.

Publisher / primary source: The Guardian · The Guardian — 7 Nov 2026 · GOV.UK — 18 Aug 2026 financing announcement

05POLICY / AI SAFETY8 NOV 2026

US AI safety centre faces an independence question as staff are reportedly drawn closer to the White House

The Commerce Department unit has no permanent director and has stopped publishing research, AP reports. The proposed reporting arrangement has not been formally announced.

The Associated Press reported on 8 November that staff at the Commerce Department’s Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI) had recently been asked to report informally to a White House office. AP attributed that account to two former government officials familiar with the discussions. The White House did not respond to AP’s specific questions about the centre.

The unit has been without a permanent director after leadership turnover and has stopped publishing public research on AI-system safety, AP reports. It has an annual allocation of $10m. Critics quoted by AP worry that closer White House control could weaken independent technical scrutiny; others argue that a higher-level reporting line might improve its visibility and funding. Those are competing interpretations, not established outcomes.

The policy question is practical: who independently tests frontier systems, publishes the results and checks whether voluntary company safety commitments are being followed? AP notes that current agent evaluations can miss harmful intermediate actions if they assess only the final result.

Evidence: AP’s reporting on the informal request relies on two former officials. The centre’s leadership gap and pause in public research are also reported by AP. No formal transfer or new legal mandate was announced.

Why developers should care: Developers deploying agents in high-impact workflows need credible external evaluation, not just vendor-authored model cards. Independent testing standards can shape procurement, audit obligations and incident reporting.

Editorial rank: Ranks fifth for its potential effect on the independence of US AI evaluation. The reporting is substantial, but the proposed White House arrangement remains informal and unconfirmed by the administration.

Publisher / primary source: Associated Press · Associated Press — 8 Nov 2026 · Washington Post — AP report, 8 Nov 2026

AI in context

Analysis · not reported fact

1. Power is joining silicon and memory as a scarce input

Anthropic’s reported generator purchase, DataVita’s revised grid plan and Southeast Asia’s project pauses point to the same constraint from different angles. A GPU order does not create usable compute until a site has reliable electricity, cooling, water, networking and permits. More AI labs may end up owning or financing pieces of the energy supply chain, taking on risks that used to sit with utilities and infrastructure developers.

2. The AI build-out is becoming a local bargain

Thailand’s review, West Java’s stop-work order and the Lanarkshire grid revision show that governments and communities can change the timetable after a project is announced. Jobs and capital commitments matter, but so do water use, grid upgrades, land and the share of long-term value retained locally. These decisions will shape where regional inference capacity is placed.

3. Trust infrastructure matters as much as model safeguards

Nvidia’s board inquiry and the CAISSI reporting-line questions are different problems, but both concern who can inspect sensitive systems and who is accountable when the stakes are national security. For developers, the practical response is traceable access, strong partner controls, independent evaluations and evidence that can be audited later.

4. Copyright rules can travel across borders

IFPI’s warning is that one permissive national exception could become a route for training on material sourced globally. Whether that argument prevails is a policy question, but product teams should already be able to explain data provenance, licensing status, opt-out handling and the jurisdictional basis for training.

Plain-English glossary

Terms used in this edition
GW
Gigawatt, one billion watts. A generator’s nameplate rating is not the same as continuous delivered power.
Behind-the-meter
Power generated on the customer’s side of the utility meter, often at or near the facility.
Private wire
A dedicated electrical connection that sends power from a generator directly to a site.
PPA
Power-purchase agreement: a contract to buy electricity, often from a named renewable project.
CAISSI
US Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence, a Commerce Department safety/evaluation unit.
FOI
Freedom of Information request: a formal request for records held by a public authority.
Agentic flooding
A surge in the volume or complexity of requests to a service because AI makes them cheap to generate.
