Editor's Briefing

India's model race has a new open-weight contender, while the AI inference supply chain is facing its clearest privacy stress test yet. Sarvam released a post-trained 105B mixture-of-experts checkpoint focused on tool use and Indian-language conversations, with Apache-2.0 weights and a sharply lower vendor-quoted token price. Separately, a Dragos canary study and provider disclosures have exposed prompt access, credential misuse and routing-policy failures across parts of the inference marketplace. OpenRouter says it has off-boarded three providers and removed 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention eligibility.

India's CERT-In has also warned that attackers are changing business records and approval chains so that legitimate payment workflows execute unauthorised transfers. The underlying critical advisory was issued on 20 November and surfaced in public reporting on 21–22 November; it is not a new incident report naming a specific victim.

Editorial note: Only three developments cleared the materiality and date-window test. The Dragos study was published on 19 November, just outside the 48-hour window; the story is included because provider disclosures and platform actions on 21–23 November materially changed the response. Sarvam benchmark and throughput claims remain vendor-run; no independent reproduction was found.

Models / India · 22 NovOpen-weight release

Sarvam releases 105B checkpoint aimed at low-cost Indian-language agents

Sarvam AI released Sarvam-105B-1711 on 22 November, a post-trained checkpoint of its 105-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model. The base model has 10.3B active parameters; the new checkpoint targets instruction following, tool calls, agentic tasks and Indian languages.

The weights are available on Hugging Face under Apache-2.0 in BF16 and NVFP4 formats. Sarvam lists ₹25 per million output tokens and reports a blended inference price of ₹13.75 (US$0.15) per million tokens. The input-token price was not separately visible in the sources checked, so no input rate is inferred here. Its own eight-benchmark conversational-agent average rose from 42.6 for the February checkpoint to 73.9. The company also reports 79.1 on its Indic suite and 85.5 on τ²-Bench.

Those are vendor-run evaluations, not an independent reproduction. Sarvam says it evaluated models using its own harness, four samples and official graders; open-weight models ran through vLLM while closed APIs were called through OpenRouter at their providers' maximum context. The harness is described, but those serving conditions are not identical. The launch blog discusses 160K-token long-context training; the published vLLM recipe sets max-model-len to 131,072, so 128K is the concrete deployment setting to plan around.

The Hugging Face model card reports a B200 run at 128K context with 122 tokens/second/user at 64 concurrent users and 156 ms p50 time-to-first-token. On a single 96 GB RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPU, its 50K-context test reports 61.8 tokens/second for one user and 8.1 seconds p50 time-to-first-token. These are vendor measurements using a specific vLLM/DSpark setup, not independent latency guarantees. The NVFP4 pack is about 65 GB; this is workstation-class hardware, not a laptop deployment. The model card also requires trust_remote_code for its serving recipe, which production teams should review.

Practical fit: Indian-language customer support, voice agents, retrieval-grounded enterprise assistants and high-volume multi-turn tool workflows where a team can host or contract for the required GPU capacity. Limits: no independent quality reproduction, high memory requirements, and benchmark comparisons that should not be read as a universal leaderboard ranking.

Why developers should care: evaluate cost per successfully completed tool workflow, not cost per token alone. Test code-mixed speech, tool arguments, retries, long conversations, concurrency and GPU memory on your own data before replacing a hosted frontier model.

Security / AI infrastructure · 21–23 NovDeveloping disclosures

AI inference supply chain faces prompt-leak disclosures; OpenRouter removes three providers

A Dragos experiment using nearly two million unique canary prompts found evidence of data exposure at at least three AI hosting providers, prompt retention by some inference-marketplace resellers and apparent failures to honour privacy settings. The research was published on 19 November; the material update in this window is the sequence of provider disclosures and OpenRouter's 22 November response.

OpenRouter says its investigation found no breach or unauthorised access of OpenRouter itself. It says it off-boarded three providers, removed 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention eligibility—including precautionary removals—and notified potentially affected customers. The company has not named the three off-boarded providers; its public list contains disclosures from Atlas Cloud, Novita, Phala and Together, which should not be assumed to be the same set.

Three separate provider disclosures illustrate different failure modes. Together AI said an AI coding agent committed a credential to a public repository during development; an unauthorised party used it to download stored prompt samples from June and July 2026 and tested credentials found in them. Five were Dragos canaries. Together says its review of prompt contents is ongoing and it has not identified evidence of additional prompt leakage beyond this incident so far.

Novita said a routing configuration issue caused some MiniMax requests to be forwarded to MiniMax's official API between 28 May and 11 November. It says request contents may have been accessed or retained by a third party, suspended the affected models and observed no use of the AWS canary-key markers. Atlas Cloud said a third party opened links embedded in prompts sent to MiniMax's official API through Atlas in Q3 and early Q4; it stopped sending traffic to that API and told users to treat secrets in those prompts as exposed. Atlas says it found no issue with its other models so far.

Phala's earlier November 10 report describes a separate server compromise: DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 requests routed to Phala by OpenRouter between 21 October and 8 November should be treated as exposed. Phala says it contained the incident on 8 November, rotated its KMS root key on 6 November and restored the service with additional protections. This is background to the wider supply-chain picture, not a new November 23 incident.

Dragos does not name the compromised providers or disclose affected customer counts. Its canary evidence is a security-research finding, not a full independent audit of every provider or every prompt. The public disclosures cover distinct windows and causes; they do not prove that all traffic on OpenRouter, all MiniMax traffic or all ZDR-labelled requests were exposed.

What customers should do: identify every router, reseller and upstream inference provider in the request path; review provider-specific retention and data-processing terms; avoid placing live credentials, passwords or private URLs in prompts; rotate any secrets that may have been included in affected requests; and use direct provider contracts or self-hosting for high-sensitivity workloads where feasible. A ZDR label is a policy promise, not proof that every downstream hop enforces it.

Why developers should care: the model endpoint is only one part of the security boundary. Log the actual upstream route, constrain provider pools, redact secrets before inference, use short-lived scoped credentials, and make vendor incident response and data-deletion commitments testable.

Security / India · 20 Nov advisory, public coverage 21–22 NovCritical advisory

CERT-In warns attackers are altering payment records and approval chains

CERT-In advisory CIAD-2026-0062, issued on 20 November and rated critical, warns that attackers are changing business records or approval relationships so that otherwise legitimate payment workflows execute unauthorised transfers. Public reporting on 21–22 November says the warning extends beyond banks to technology service providers supporting government-linked payment processes and platforms handling vendor or partner payouts.

The advisory describes AI-driven agentic offensive frameworks used for reconnaissance, privilege discovery and parallel intrusion tasks. The important distinction is that the payment may pass normal application checks because the underlying beneficiary or approval data has been altered. Securing the payment API alone will not catch a transaction that is syntactically valid but based on a compromised business record.

CERT-In's reported recommendations include independent verification of payment instructions and beneficiary changes, separation of client environments, tighter administrative and control-plane privileges, and monitoring record changes together with privileged activity and subsequent transactions. The public reports do not name affected organisations or disclose loss amounts. The official advisory URL timed out during this research; the details below are corroborated by multiple outlets quoting the advisory, not by a directly readable primary page.

Why developers should care: ERP, procurement, vendor-management and payout systems need controls around the data that drives approval—not only authentication at the API gateway. Use independent reconciliation, dual approval for beneficiary changes, short-lived credentials, immutable audit trails and alerting that correlates record edits with payment execution.

Three analysis observations

AI in Context

01 / Cheap tokens are not cheap tasks

Sarvam's vendor-reported price is attractive, but teams should benchmark the full workflow: task success, tool-call retries, latency under concurrency, GPU memory, language coverage and human escalation. Cost per completed customer request is the useful unit.

02 / The route is part of the model

An inference router can improve model choice and billing, but every hop that decrypts a prompt becomes a data processor. ZDR at the first hop cannot constrain a reseller, compromised host or downstream provider that never honours the flag.

03 / Business records are an attack surface

CERT-In's warning points beyond API authentication. If an attacker changes the beneficiary or approval record, the payment system may execute a valid-looking transaction. Independent verification and reconciliation matter as much as gateway security.

Terms used in this edition

glossary

Mixture of experts (MoE): a model architecture that routes each token through only a subset of its expert networks. Total parameters can be much larger than the active parameters used per token.

Active parameters: the subset of model weights engaged for a given token or forward pass; not the total stored model size.

NVFP4: Nvidia's four-bit floating-point format used to compress model weights and reduce memory requirements.

Time to first token (TTFT): elapsed time between sending a request and receiving the first generated token.

Zero Data Retention (ZDR): a provider policy that says prompts and responses are not stored. It must be verified across every processor and route, not assumed from one API flag.

Inference router: a service that forwards a prompt to one or more model providers, often for unified billing, availability or model selection.

Canary token: a unique decoy credential or URL that signals exposure when someone accesses or uses it.

Agentic offensive framework: a toolchain that automates reconnaissance, privilege discovery and other intrusion tasks using one or more coordinated agents.
